Wi-Fi Association, Security & Roaming

Seeing a network, joining a radio, passing authentication, and reaching an application are four different milestones.

Short answer: a client discovers a BSSID, negotiates an association and security, then obtains or reuses IP configuration. Roaming changes its attachment to another BSSID. The client normally decides when to roam; 802.11k, 802.11r, and 802.11v assist different parts of that process.

Read bands and airtime, DHCP, and certificate trust first.

An exhibition pass, not just a building name

At a trade exhibition, seeing the event's name doesn't admit you. You choose an entrance, present a pass, clear the admission check, and find the stand you need. The event name is the SSID; a particular entrance is a BSSID; association establishes the radio relationship; security establishes authorized protected access; DHCP and DNS help you reach your destination. A copied event sign doesn't prove an entrance is genuine, just as an SSID alone doesn't authenticate a network.

Joining: identify the last successful stage

  1. Discovery: the client learns about BSSIDs from beacons and, where applicable, probe exchanges. Capabilities and the RSN information element advertise supported security. Discovery behavior differs by band, especially at 6 GHz.
  2. 802.11 authentication and association: the client and AP exchange management frames. Open System authentication used in WPA2 networks is not password validation. WPA3-Personal uses SAE authentication before association.
  3. Security and keys: enterprise networks typically run 802.1X/EAP authentication, often through a RADIUS service. An ordinary full WPA2/WPA3 connection also establishes traffic keys with the four-way handshake. A shared password is not transmitted as plaintext in that handshake.
  4. IP and service access: IPv4 may use DHCP, followed by gateway/ARP and DNS checks. IPv6 uses its own router-discovery and address-configuration mechanisms. A successful association doesn't prove any of these later stages succeeded.

Know what each security mode authenticates

ModeHow access is establishedOperational consequence
WPA2-Personal with AES-CCMPA pre-shared secret contributes to key establishmentShared-secret distribution and rotation are difficult; weak passphrases are vulnerable to offline guessing from suitable captured exchanges
WPA3-PersonalSAE is a password-authenticated key exchange; PMF is requiredImproves resistance to passive offline dictionary attacks; still needs strong credentials, supported clients, and updates
WPA2/WPA3-Enterprise802.1X with an EAP method, usually backed by RADIUSSupports individual identities and policy; EAP-TLS uses certificates and requires a working certificate lifecycle

At the exhibition, a shared invitation code is different from a named pass verified against an issuer. For EAP-TLS, validate the authentication server's certificate and expected name, as well as the client certificate. Don't teach users to accept an unexpected certificate prompt. A RADIUS accept can carry an assigned VLAN, so authentication success followed by no DHCP lease may be a VLAN or DHCP-path fault.

Protected Management Frames (PMF) protect selected robust management frames, including protected deauthentication/disassociation after keys exist. They don't prevent RF interference, protect every management frame, or make an evil-twin SSID impossible. WPA2/WPA3 transition mode retains a legacy path; it isn't equivalent to a WPA3-only deployment. Wi-Fi certification for 6 GHz requires newer security modes such as WPA3 or Enhanced Open rather than legacy WPA2-only operation.

A captive portal is an application sign-in after basic connectivity, not a replacement for wireless encryption or server identity checks. In the exhibition, a survey inside the hall isn't the entrance's identity check.

Roaming is a client decision with assistance

MechanismWhat it helps withExhibition equivalent
802.11kNeighbor reports help the client find candidate BSSIDs/channelsA directory of nearby entrances
802.11vBSS transition management can suggest alternatives; the client evaluates themStaff recommend a less crowded entrance
802.11rFast BSS Transition reduces security handoff work within the configured mobility domainParticipating entrances recognize a securely derived admission credential

The client weighs signal, candidates, driver policy, and sometimes load. APs can influence selection, but a forced disconnection is not a guarantee of a smooth roam. Vendor thresholds such as Apple's documented triggers describe particular clients, not a universal -70 dBm law. Test legacy scanners and voice clients before changing FT or PMF requirements.

Worked example: a call crosses two APs

A handset on SSID Staff moves from BSSID 02:00:00:00:07:01 to 02:00:00:00:07:02. In this synthetic example, it remains in VLAN 20 and keeps 10.20.0.42/24. The application connection may survive a short handoff without a new DHCP lease. Record the old/new BSSID, authentication result, IP address, and packet-delivery gap to establish what happened.

If the second AP maps Staff to a different subnet, an identical SSID doesn't preserve IP reachability by itself. A supported mobility tunnel/anchor design may preserve addressing, or the client may need new IP configuration. At the exhibition, another entrance bearing the same event sign must still lead into the same admission system and hall.

TEST THE HANDOFF, NOT JUST THE SIGNAL

Walk the actual route with the affected client and application. A single signal reading cannot establish roaming quality, and a successful ping after the roam doesn't measure the interruption during it.
