Copper, Fiber, Transceivers & PoE

The port, cable, optics, and power source form one system. A plug that fits is only the first compatibility check.

Short answer: twisted-pair copper carries electrical signals and can supply Power over Ethernet (PoE). Fiber carries light and suits longer links or electrical isolation. A transceiver must match both its host port and the link at the other end. PoE needs enough power per port and across the whole switch.

Start with network layers and full-duplex Ethernet.

A stage rig needs more than matching plugs

Setting up a concert stage means checking signal cables, adapters, run length, and the electrical supply. A microphone connector doesn't tell you whether it belongs in a speaker output, and enough sockets don't guarantee enough power. Ethernet has the same separate questions: 1000BASE-T describes a signaling technology; Cat6A describes cabling; SFP+ describes a pluggable interface family; 802.3at describes PoE capabilities.

A conventional structured-cabling channel includes patch cords, not just the cable inside the wall. A common design allows 90 m of permanent link plus 10 m of patching, subject to the applicable cabling rules. Cat6 can carry 10GBASE-T over shorter qualified runs; don't treat it as a blanket 100 m guarantee. Likewise, 2.5GBASE-T and 5GBASE-T depend on supported ports and a suitable installed channel.

On stage, a long signal run may need a different medium rather than another adapter. Fiber avoids electrical signal coupling between endpoints, but it still needs correctly rated optics and clean connectors. Fiber doesn't deliver ordinary PoE; a remote fiber-connected access point needs local power or a separate powered copper segment.

Match both ends of an optical link

Small Form-factor Pluggable (SFP) modules commonly serve 1 Gbit/s links; SFP+ commonly serves 10 Gbit/s and SFP28 commonly serves 25 Gbit/s. These names don't promise that a port accepts every lower-speed module. Check the exact switch model, software, supported speed, and transceiver matrix, just as a stage adapter must suit the equipment behind its socket.

  1. Match signaling and fiber: an SR optic and an LR optic aren't a compatible pair simply because both use LC connectors.
  2. Check transmit/receive paths: ordinary duplex fiber needs Tx at one end connected to Rx at the other. Single-fiber bidirectional optics require complementary wavelength pairs, not two identical ends.
  3. Check connector type and polish: LC and MPO describe different connectors. Don't mate APC and UPC ends or assume cable color proves the installed fiber grade.
  4. Check optical levels: digital optical monitoring (DOM) reports transmit/receive power, often in dBm. Compare it with the module's limits. Too little light and receiver overload are both problems.

For a synthetic budget example, a transmitter minimum of -3 dBm and receiver sensitivity of -10 dBm leave 7 dB before margin. If connectors, splices, and fiber consume 4 dB, 3 dB remains. Also check maximum transmit power against receiver overload; this subtraction alone doesn't certify a link. Never look into a fiber end. Use approved inspection and cleaning equipment with the link made safe.

PoE is a negotiated budget

The PSE (power sourcing equipment) is a switch or injector; the PD (powered device) is an AP, phone, or camera. Standards-based PoE detects a compatible PD before applying operating power. Classification and, where supported, LLDP help determine the allocation. A passive injector may apply power without IEEE detection, so it isn't an interchangeable substitute.

These are type ceilings and highest-class PD figures, not a promise that every port or PD supports them. Cable losses explain the PSE/PD difference. Higher-power four-pair PoE also makes cable quality, bundle temperature, and installation limits important. Like the stage's electrical distribution, count the required allocations, not just the sockets.

Worked example: eight access points

Suppose a switch has a 370 W available PoE budget, and each of eight AP ports reserves 30 W at the PSE. Each AP must also fit within the PD power available from that class. The switch reserves 240 W, leaving 130 W before other devices and your chosen reserve.

Check the budget after a power-supply failure too. If the remaining supply supports only 185 W of PoE, it cannot maintain all eight 30 W reservations. A redundant supply isn't automatically enough for full-load redundancy. Vendor power-priority policy determines which ports remain powered.

LABEL THE WHOLE LINK

Record both port IDs, both optic part numbers, fiber grade, patch path, and power allocation. A link light confirms a physical connection, not the correct VLAN, IP address, or application reachability.
