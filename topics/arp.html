ARP

A device knows the IP address it wants to reach. It has no idea what MAC address that is. ARP is the one-question, one-answer exchange that fixes that.

The problem ARP solves

Every Ethernet frame needs a destination MAC address before it can be sent, but applications and routing tables only ever deal in IP addresses. Something has to bridge that gap on the local segment. That's the Address Resolution Protocol: given an IP address, find the MAC address of whatever device is actually holding it.

It works like calling out across an open-plan office: "Who has desk assignment 10.0.0.5?" You don't know or care who that is yet, you just want them to identify themselves. Whoever holds that assignment calls back directly: "That's me, and here's exactly which desk to walk to."

Request and reply

  • ARP request: sent as a Layer 2 broadcast (destination MAC FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF), meaning "who has 10.0.0.5? tell 10.0.0.1." Every device on the segment receives it, but only the device that owns that IP responds.
  • ARP reply: sent as a unicast straight back to the requester: "10.0.0.5 is at MAC AA:BB:CC:11:22:33."

The ARP cache

Doing this exchange for every single packet would be wasteful, so each device keeps an ARP cache: a table of recently resolved IP-to-MAC mappings with a timeout (commonly a few minutes, varying by OS). As long as an entry is cached, the device skips straight to sending the frame.

A device can also send a gratuitous ARP, an unsolicited announcement of its own IP-to-MAC mapping, typically right after it boots or changes its address. Every other device on the segment updates its cache immediately instead of waiting for someone to ask.

WHY ARP NEVER CROSSES A ROUTER

ARP is a broadcast, and routers don't forward broadcasts between networks — that's exactly what makes them a broadcast domain boundary (see Collision Domain vs Broadcast Domain). To reach a device on another network, a host ARPs for its default gateway's MAC address instead, and lets routing take over from there.
