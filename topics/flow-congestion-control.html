Flow Control & Congestion Control

Two different problems, both solved by TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) dynamically shrinking or growing how much data is allowed to be in flight at once.

Two different problems, two different mechanisms

It's easy to lump these together since both end up controlling "how much can I send right now," but they're solving completely different problems, and one of them is told directly while the other has to be guessed.

Picture dictating a letter to someone writing it by hand, over a shared phone line other calls are also using. Flow control is your listener saying "stop, wait, my hand's cramping" whenever they need to. You don't send more words until they say go again: a direct, explicit signal. Congestion control is different. Nobody tells you the phone line itself is getting crowded. You just notice the line crackling or words cutting out and, on your own, decide to slow down until it clears: an inferred signal, based on what you observe, not what anyone reports.

Flow control: The receive window

Every TCP segment carries a Window Size field (see the TCP header): how many more bytes the sender of that segment can currently accept, based on how much space is actually free in its receive buffer. If a fast sender is about to overrun a slow receiver's buffer, the receiver just advertises a smaller window, and the sender is required to respect it. A window of 0 means "stop sending entirely," and the sender has to periodically probe with a small segment until the receiver advertises room again.

Congestion control: What the sender infers on its own

The receive window says nothing about the network path in between, which might be far more congested than the receiver's buffer ever is. For that, the sender keeps its own internal value, the congestion window (cwnd), that never appears in any header field and is never advertised to anyone. It starts small and grows based on what the sender infers from how segments are being acknowledged, and shrinks fast the moment it infers loss.

  • Slow start: cwnd starts at a small number of segments (historically as low as 1, larger in modern stacks) and roughly doubles every round-trip, growing exponentially, until it hits a threshold called ssthresh.
  • Congestion avoidance: past ssthresh, growth switches to roughly linear, one segment's worth of extra room per round-trip, a much more cautious climb.
  • Loss response: three duplicate ACKs in a row usually mean a single segment was dropped, not a serious jam. TCP does fast retransmit, sends that segment again immediately, and cuts cwnd roughly in half rather than starting over from scratch.
  • Timeout response: no ACK at all for a segment is treated as a much worse sign. cwnd drops all the way back down and slow start restarts from the beginning.
THE ACTUAL LIMIT IS WHICHEVER WINDOW IS SMALLER

A sender never gets to use more than min(receive window, congestion window) worth of unacknowledged data at once. A fast local network with a slow, congested path to the destination will be limited by cwnd; a fast path to a receiver with a tiny buffer will be limited by the receive window. Either one alone tells an incomplete story.
